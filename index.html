Aligning Latent Geometry for Spherical Flow Matching in Image Generation

🚀 TL;DR

Latent flow matching ignores the geometry of VAE latents. Both Gaussian noise and VAE latent tokens concentrate on thin spherical shells, yet linear flow matching transports them with a straight line that leaves those shells and spends much of its training signal on radial motion the decoder barely uses. We project each VAE token onto a fixed-radius sphere, finetune only the decoder, and replace linear paths with spherical linear interpolation (slerp). The diffusion architecture is unchanged, no auxiliary encoder is needed, and class-conditional ImageNet-256 FID improves across FLUX.2, VA-VAE, and REPA-E FLUX.1 tokenizers at both SiT-B and SiT-XL scales.

🤔 Motivation

Latent diffusion models do not generate pixels directly; they model the coordinate system produced by a pretrained VAE. The shape of that latent distribution defines the space the generator must traverse, yet standard latent flow matching treats it as plain Euclidean space and transports Gaussian noise to encoded data along straight lines. This overlooks two structural facts about VAE latents: their tokens concentrate in thin spherical shells, and the decoder reacts mainly to a token's direction rather than its length. Flow matching pays for both in image quality.

VAE tokens and Gaussian noise both live on thin shells

In high dimensions, samples from \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) concentrate near a shell of radius \(\sqrt{d}\), with relative thickness \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{d})\). Encoded VAE tokens behave the same way: across FLUX.2, VA-VAE, and REPA-E FLUX.1, per-token norms have a coefficient of variation under 0.23 both raw and after each pipeline's preprocessing. After preprocessing, FLUX.2 and VA-VAE sit close to the Gaussian shell, while REPA-E FLUX.1 sits well below. Spherical projection collapses all three to \(\bar r / \sqrt d = 1\) with zero variation: preprocessing is partial, projection is universal.

Figure 2. Linear paths can dip away from the endpoint shells; shell paths interpolate token radii; spherical-slerp stays on the fixed-radius sphere at every timestep.

The decoder reads direction, not length

We probe what part of a latent token the decoder actually uses. For an anchor token \(z_a = r_a \hat z_a\) and a same-position token \(z_n = r_n \hat z_n\) from a same-class neighbor, we form two hybrids: anchor direction, neighbor radius (\(r_n \hat z_a\)) and anchor radius, neighbor direction (\(r_a \hat z_n\)). Keeping the anchor direction leaves the decoded image close to the anchor; keeping only the anchor radius moves it almost as far as replacing the whole latent. The asymmetry shows up on both LPIPS and DINOv2 distances.

Linear flow matching spends supervision on radial motion

The per-token velocity target \(u_t = \dot z_t\) decomposes into a radial part (changing the token's distance from the origin) and a tangential part (changing its direction). Under linear flow matching this radial share accounts for roughly half of the training signal for FLUX.2 and VA-VAE near the endpoints, and reaches about 90% at the noise endpoint for REPA-E FLUX.1. Slerp on the sphere makes the radial share identically zero by construction.

🗺️ Method

Our recipe has two parts. First, we make the VAE latent space spherical by inserting a token-wise \(L_2\) projection at the encoder output and finetuning only the decoder. Second, we train flow matching along spherical-linear-interpolation (slerp) geodesics, with the velocity target and the model output projected onto the tangent space of the sphere.

Spherical latent projection

Given a pretrained encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) with token dimension \(d\), define \(\pi:\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt d)\) by \(\pi(z) = \sqrt d\, z/\|z\|\) and apply it independently at each spatial position:

\( z_{i,j} \leftarrow \sqrt{d} \cdot \dfrac{z_{i,j}}{\|z_{i,j}\|}, \quad \|z_{i,j}\| = \sqrt{d}. \)

The radius \(\sqrt d\) matches the concentration radius of a standard Gaussian in \(d\) dimensions, aligning the projected latent scale with the noise prior. We freeze the encoder, insert the projection at its output, and finetune the decoder and discriminator with the original pixel \(L_1\), LPIPS, and patch-adversarial losses. The KL term is dropped (the encoder is frozen and the projected latent is deterministic), and for VA-VAE the DINOv2 alignment terms are removed (their gradient through a frozen encoder is zero). We finetune for five epochs on ImageNet.

Slerp transport on the sphere

With both endpoints on \(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt d)\), the unique shortest geodesic between them is the spherical-linear-interpolation arc:

\( z_t \;=\; \sqrt{d}\left( \dfrac{\sin((1-t)\omega)}{\sin\omega}\,\hat z_0 + \dfrac{\sin(t\omega)}{\sin\omega}\,\hat z_1 \right), \qquad \omega = \arccos\langle \hat z_0, \hat z_1 \rangle. \)

For the noise endpoint we use the radial projection of an isotropic Gaussian: \(z_0 = \sqrt d\,\epsilon/\| \epsilon\|\) with \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\). By rotational invariance, \(z_0\) is uniform on \(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt d)\), so the spherical noise endpoint is the angular component of the very same Gaussian prior used by the linear baseline.

The conditional velocity field is the time derivative of \(z_t\), projected onto the tangent space \(T_{z_t}\mathcal{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt d)\):

\( u_t = \dot z_t, \qquad u_t \leftarrow u_t - \dfrac{\langle u_t, z_t \rangle}{\|z_t\|^2}\, z_t. \)

In exact arithmetic, the slerp time-derivative already lies in the tangent space; the projection is a numerical safeguard on the target and a hard tangency constraint on the model output \(v_\theta\). The training loss is the standard flow-matching objective with both sides projected to the tangent space:

\( \mathcal{L}_{\text{slerp}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,z_0,\,z_1}\!\left[\big\|\Pi_{z_t} v_\theta(z_t, t, y) - \Pi_{z_t}\, u_t\big\|^2\right], \quad \Pi_{z_t} v = v - \dfrac{\langle v, z_t\rangle}{\|z_t\|^2} z_t. \)

Sphere-preserving sampler

At inference we integrate with the exponential map on the sphere: \(z_{t+\Delta t} = \exp_{z_t}\!\big(\Pi_{z_t}\, v_\theta(z_t, t, y)\,\Delta t\big)\). This step stays on the sphere by construction and has a closed-form trigonometric expression, adding negligible cost. The sampler is matched to the slerp training target: a single exponential-map step with a perfect predictor lands exactly on the next slerp point, whereas an Euler step followed by radial renormalization advances only by \(\sqrt d\arctan(\Delta t\,\omega)\) and suffers a one-step deficit.

What's not changed

The diffusion backbone (SiT) sees the spherical latents as ordinary vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and needs no architectural changes. No auxiliary encoder is used during diffusion training or inference, unlike methods that diffuse in the feature space of a frozen DINOv2. The projection composes with both representation-aligned (VA-VAE, REPA-E FLUX.1) and non-aligned (FLUX.2) tokenizers.

📈 Results

Ablation: latent support & transport path

On SiT-B/2 with the FLUX.2 VAE, the spherical projection alone explains most of the gain over vanilla-linear flow matching; the slerp path on top gives the best result. Shell-decomposed transport on vanilla latents does not improve the baseline: avoiding the inward chord dip is insufficient if the network is still asked to predict both angular and radial velocity under a single objective.

Longer training: the lead persists

Extending FLUX.2 B/2 to 200 epochs at the same batch size confirms the advantage is not a short-budget artifact. Both methods improve, and spherical-slerp remains ahead at both guidance settings.

Reconstruction cost is modest

The fixed-radius projection introduces a small reconstruction penalty relative to a finetuned-vanilla control, but the downstream FID improves substantially. Downstream generation always decodes through the matching spherical decoder, so the gain cannot be attributed to a stronger decoder — a decoder-swap control rules this out in both directions.
